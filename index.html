Historical background
  • The Pullman Company made railroad sleeping cars near Chicago and ran a company town where many workers rented homes.
  • During the economic downturn of the 1890s, wages were cut. Workers argued that rent and other living costs did not fall as much as wages.
  • Workers went on strike in 1894. The American Railway Union backed them with a wider boycott involving trains carrying Pullman cars.
  • Rail traffic was disrupted. Federal intervention and court action became major parts of the conflict. Violence occurred in some locations during the broader strike and boycott.
  • Historical interpretations differ over responsibility, federal power, workers’ demands, union strategy, and what the conflict meant.

Company town: A town run by a company. Boycott: An organized refusal to deal with something as a form of protest.

Access to events
The writer knows the household’s finances firsthand and has limited knowledge of company-wide finances or negotiations.

Company statement

Simulated composite for learning

The company says falling demand reduced revenue and argues that wage cuts were necessary to keep operations running. It says rental properties are separate from wage decisions and emphasizes past investments in housing and community facilities.

Context for the Company statement
Form, audience, and purpose
A public statement distributed to newspapers to defend company decisions.
Access to events
It presents the company’s own decisions and explanations. It may contain useful information about operations and is also meant to persuade.

Newspaper account

Simulated composite for learning

A Chicago reporter describes delayed trains, crowds near rail yards, and passengers frustrated by disrupted service. The report quotes a railroad manager who blames union leaders and briefly mentions workers’ complaints about wages.

Context for the Newspaper account
Form, audience, and purpose
A newspaper report for a citywide readership, rather than primarily Pullman workers. It reports on rail disruptions.
Access to events
The reporter observes some events firsthand and relies heavily on railroad officials. The claim about union leaders comes from a railroad manager.

Reform investigator’s account

Simulated composite for learning

A social reformer records conversations with several worker families, notes small homes and household budgets, and questions whether company-town arrangements give workers enough independence. The notes also acknowledge that some residents praise the town’s cleanliness and services.

Context for the Reform investigator’s account
Form, audience, and purpose
Field notes by an investigator sympathetic to reform who seeks evidence about living conditions. The materials do not specify an audience.
Access to events
The notes draw on observations and interviews with several families. They are not a representative survey.

Check your comparison

The public purpose of the Company statement does not rule out useful information about company decisions. The Worker’s account offers a firsthand view of household pressures. The Newspaper account combines observation with a railroad manager’s explanation.

Corroborate means to check a claim against other evidence. If the Company statement and Newspaper account agree, consider where each got its information before counting the agreement as independent support.
